Shared Inputs

Four ways labels work

Personal

They give language

MBTI is described by its publisher as a framework for preferences and interpersonal differences.

Source: The Myers-Briggs Company, MBTI Facts.

Social

They connect us to groups

Social identity theory studies how group membership can become part of a person’s self-concept.

Source: Tajfel & Turner, 1979.

Ambiguous

They can feel surprisingly accurate

In Forer’s 1949 study, students rated a vague personality profile as highly accurate: 4.26 out of 5 on average.

Source: Bertram R. Forer, 1949.

Playful

They are often entertainment, too

A 2024 Pew survey found 30% of U.S. adults consult astrology, tarot, or fortune tellers at least once a year, often just for fun.

Source: Pew Research Center, 2025 report.

Useful Language

Words that keep the discussion open

self-label

a word you choose for yourself

assigned label

a word others put on you

stereotype

a fixed idea about a group

context

the situation around a label

agency

the ability to choose or change

Try: “In my experience...”, “It depends on...”, “That label can be useful when...”, “It becomes unfair when...”

Round 1 · Everyday Experience

Round 1: personal stories first

Start small

A first meeting, a team intro, a family joke, a school memory, a dating profile, a hometown comment, an online bio.

Group rhythm

Pick one question. Each person shares a short example, then the group looks for patterns and differences.

Round 1 · 4 Group Questions

Start from real life, not right answers

Question 1

What is a label you sometimes use for yourself? In what situation does it feel useful?

Question 2

When has someone guessed something about you from a label? What did they get right or miss?

Question 3

What is a label people around you often joke about? Why does it feel funny, friendly, or uncomfortable?

Question 4

Which label changes meaning depending on who says it, where they say it, or how well they know you?

Round 1 · Extend

After everyone shares, compare the stories

Pattern

Repeating patterns

Notice shared labels, feelings, or situations across different stories.

Difference

Changing meanings

The same label can shift across age, region, job, relationship, or tone.

Feeling

Emotional tone

Pride, relief, pressure, humor, distance, curiosity, or resistance.

Try saying

“For me, it means...”

This phrase helps move from a fixed label to a personal story.

Round 2 · Question 1

Why do labels become so common?

Cognitive shortcut

We group information to reduce complexity and make fast sense of people.

Belonging

Labels can signal “people like me” and make identity easier to share.

Social script

Groups repeat familiar categories: hometown, job, age, MBTI, zodiac, school.

Efficiency

Labels save time in introductions, matching, hiring, dating, and online profiles.

Small-group task: rank these explanations from most convincing to least convincing, then add one explanation your group thinks is missing.

Round 2 · Question 2

Do labels help us or limit us?

HELP

Labels give language, belonging, confidence, and useful starting points.

They can help people explain preferences, needs, boundaries, and background faster.

LIMIT

Labels create assumptions, stereotypes, and frozen first impressions.

They can reduce curiosity and make it harder for people to change or be complex.

Mini debate: split into two sides for 4 minutes. Use examples from Round 1, then switch sides and make the strongest argument for the opposite view.

Round 2 · Question 3

How can we use labels without putting people in a box?

Add context

“For me, it means...” “It helps explain...” “It does not mean...”

Ask better

Ask for the story behind the label before assuming the meaning.

Make it optional

Let people choose whether to use a label, explain it, skip it, or change it.

Keep updating

Leave room for people to grow, contradict themselves, or surprise you later.

Group challenge: create two useful sentences: one for introducing yourself, and one for responding to someone else’s label.
